Who pollutes, who pays?

A visual analytics project about climate responsibility, economic capacity, energy transition gaps, and electricity access across countries from 2014 to 2023.

Period 2014-2023
Unit Country-year
Focus Climate pressure

Central question

Which countries face the strongest climate-action pressure when emissions, wealth, renewables, and access are viewed together?

We do not treat emissions as a single number. The project compares pollution intensity with economic capacity and energy-system context, so a country can be read as a profile rather than a ranking alone.

Emissions intensity GHG per person
Capacity to act GDP per capita PPP
Transition gap Renewable share
Development context Electricity access

Project story

From country diagnosis to global climate pressure

The page is organized like a guided report: first the question, then the data, then the tasks, and finally the interactive visualizations.

Why this matters

Climate debates often collapse into one question: who emits the most? That is important, but incomplete. A fairer interpretation also asks who emits more per person, who has more economic capacity, who still depends on fossil energy, and who has basic electricity access.

Target audience

The audience is non-expert readers, students, and policy-curious users who need a clear way to compare countries without reading a technical report. The interface starts with simple explanations and then offers richer interactive views.

Design direction

The visual style is warm and editorial: large country names, clear cards, restrained color, and a small number of strong interactions. The goal is to feel like a coherent story, not a folder of charts.

Limitations and bias

What the data can hide

Missing values are not random. Some countries are absent from OECD or World Bank indicators, and renewable-energy coverage becomes weaker in recent years. This can make some countries look less comparable than others.

What the metrics simplify

GDP per capita is only a proxy for capacity. Emissions per capita does not show historical responsibility, exported consumption, or within-country inequality. The visualizations should support interpretation, not replace context.

Task analysis

What the system should help users do

These tasks shaped the interface: every visualization should answer a specific question instead of simply displaying another variable.

  1. 01

    Choose a country and understand its emissions profile in plain language.

  2. 02

    Compare a country against its regional and global averages for a selected year.

  3. 03

    See whether emissions intensity is moving up or down across time.

  4. 04

    Identify countries where pressure is high because several dimensions align.

  5. 05

    Recognize where missing data or metric choices may limit the interpretation.

Animated scatter timeline

Follow how countries move through the relationship between wealth and emissions intensity from 2014 to 2023.

GHG versus wealth over time

Each dot is a country. Horizontal position shows GDP per capita, vertical position shows GHG emissions per capita, color reinforces emissions intensity, and dot size represents population.

The vertical axis is capped at 25 t CO2e per person so the main country pattern stays readable. A few extreme outliers above that value are intentionally clipped.

Use the year slider or play button inside the chart. Hover on a country to read the exact values without leaving the page.

Conclusion

Main findings so far

01

Responsibility is multidimensional

A high-emitting country is not always the largest total emitter, and a wealthy country may have more capacity to change even when its current profile looks moderate.

02

Regional context changes the story

The same country can look close to the global average but very different from its region. That is why the profile card keeps both comparisons visible.

03

Energy indicators need careful reading

Renewable share and electricity access are useful, but uneven coverage and near-universal access in wealthy regions mean they should be interpreted as context, not as a simple score.
