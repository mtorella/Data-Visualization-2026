Data Visualisation 2025/2026

Who pollutes, who pays?

An interactive data visualisation system that deconstructs global GHG emissions across geography, wealth, energy access, and measurement framing. 90 countries, 2014–2023.

Period 2014–2023
Countries 90
Sources OECD and World Bank

Four research questions

Where are emissions highest? Why do some countries emit more? Are low-emission countries truly clean? Does the metric change who looks guilty?

Public discourse often conflates low emissions with environmental virtue and high emissions with negligence, a framing that obscures the structural drivers of national carbon footprints.

Where — Spatial distribution of emissions
Why — Wealth as a driver of emissions intensity
How — Energy poverty vs deliberate decarbonisation
Different metric — Different narrative

Project story

From spatial description to ethical reflection

The system forms a coherent analytical arc: first where emissions concentrate, then why, then whether low emissions are genuinely sustainable, and finally whether the metric itself changes the verdict.

Why this matters

Climate debates often reduce responsibility to a single number. A fairer interpretation also asks who emits more per person, who has the economic capacity to act, who still depends on fossil energy, and who lacks basic electricity access: because low emissions do not always mean clean choices.

Target audience

The system targets an informed general audience: policy students, journalists, and engaged citizens with an interest in climate issues but no assumed statistical background. Design decisions consistently prioritised readability over analytical complexity.

Design direction

Each visual form was chosen to match its task directly. The interface starts with broad spatial and comparative views, then narrows to individual country profiles, and concludes with a creative synthesis of climate-action pressure across all dimensions.

Dataset description

Four public datasets joined into one country-year panel

Final working file: df_panel.csv — 789 country-year observations across 90 countries and 11 variables, 2014–2023.

OECD GHG Emissions

GHG per capita in kg CO₂-equivalent per person, filtered to total emissions excluding LULUCF and restricted to country-level observations.

Primary anchor for the join

World Bank Economy & Growth

GDP per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP), chosen over nominal GDP because PPP corrections make cross-country comparisons of living standards more meaningful.

Economic capacity signal

World Bank Energy & Mining

Renewable energy share, electricity access as a percentage of population, and fossil fuel consumption share: three complementary signals of a country's energy system.

Transition and access signals

World Bank Population

Total population counts used to derive absolute emission totals in megatonnes CO₂-equivalent, enabling the per-capita versus absolute comparison in the white-hat/black-hat views.

Scale and footprint signal

Limitations and bias

What the data can hide

The OECD GHG dataset covers only 90 countries, predominantly high- and upper-middle-income economies, introducing a systematic selection bias. Large parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia appear only partially or not at all. Missing values concentrate in small states and in energy indicators after 2021 as World Bank reporting lags increase.

Metric simplifications

What the metrics simplify

The renewable energy share variable includes traditional biomass, which can inflate the renewable share of low-income countries without reflecting a genuine clean-energy transition. Electricity access as a proxy for energy poverty is imperfect: a country may have near-universal grid access while still being economically poor. The GHG measure excludes land-use and forestry emissions. Finally, note that the project is descriptive and exploratory rather than causal.

Task analysis

Four analytical tasks in increasing depth

Each task corresponds directly to one or more visualisations. The interface is designed to support exploration at all four levels.

01

Overview

Form a spatial model of where per-capita emissions are highest and how the global distribution has shifted from 2014 to 2023.

02

Comparison

Examine whether wealthier countries systematically emit more and identify which countries break the expected pattern in either direction.

03

Explanation

Distinguish countries with low emissions by deliberate decarbonisation from those where low figures reflect energy poverty.

04

Critical reflection

Recognise how the same dataset produces opposite geopolitical narratives depending on different chart choices.

GHG versus wealth over time

Each dot is a country. This time horizontal position shows GDP per capita on a log scale, so equal distances represent equal percentage differences in wealth, not equal dollar amounts. Vertical position shows GHG emissions per capita, color reinforces emissions intensity, and dot size represents population.

The vertical axis is capped at 35 t CO₂e per person to keep the main country pattern readable. Brunei, the highest emitter in the dataset (~34 t in 2020–2021), sits just below the ceiling.

Key findings

Climate responsibility cannot be reduced to a single metric

01

Wealth explains some, not all

Wealthier countries tend to emit more per person, yet this relationship is far from uniform. Countries with similarly high GDP per capita display different emissions profiles, suggesting that development alone does not determine climate pressure.

02

Scale and per capita tell opposite stories

Some countries have very high per-capita emissions but relatively small total footprints, while others combine moderate per-capita values with very large aggregate emissions due to population size.

03

Low emissions ≠ green transition

Low emissions should not automatically be read as evidence of successful transition. In several lower-income contexts they coexist with more limited economic capacity.
